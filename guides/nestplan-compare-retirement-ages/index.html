Compare Retirement Ages with NestPlan's What If Sandbox

NestPlan guides · By CoreForge LLC · Published November 5, 2026

Retirement age is rarely one fixed decision. It is a tradeoff between more years of earning and saving, and more years of freedom. The what if sandbox in NestPlan exists so you can view that tradeoff in numbers instead of guessing. This guide explains how to set up comparisons that are actually fair, and how to read the answer.

What the sandbox does

NestPlan lets you build two or three full scenarios side by side, for example retiring at 60 versus 65 versus 70, or the same retirement age with a different withdrawal rate. You can then compare the resulting balance curves and the success probabilities directly, instead of re running the calculator from scratch for each "what if" and trying to remember the earlier answer.

A few details are worth knowing up front. The Free version of the app allows one saved scenario, and PRO allows up to 12. The success probabilities come from the Monte Carlo simulation, which is a PRO feature. If you are on the Free version, you can still learn a lot by running the projection with different inputs and noting the differences yourself. The method below works either way.

Start with a baseline you trust

Before you compare anything, make your base scenario as accurate as you can. Enter your accounts by type, your expected yearly contributions, Social Security and any pension income, and a realistic retirement spending figure. If you have not done that yet, read Getting Started with NestPlan first. A comparison between two scenarios built on shaky numbers is not much better than a comparison of guesses. What matters most is that both scenarios share the same shaky numbers, so the difference between them is still informative.

Change one thing at a time

The most common mistake in scenario planning is changing several inputs at once. If your 60 scenario also has lower spending and a higher return than your 65 scenario, you will not know which change made the difference. Follow this simple discipline:

  1. Create a second scenario that starts from exactly the same numbers as your baseline.
  2. Change only the retirement age.
  3. Note the differences in your final balance, the age at which money would run out if it does, and the success probability if you have access to it.
  4. Put the baseline back and test a different input, such as spending or the assumed return.

Treat each comparison as a small experiment with one variable. After a handful of experiments you will know which levers matter most for your situation.

What changes when you retire later

When you move your retirement age later, several things shift together, even though you only changed one input.

  • More years of contributions. You keep adding to your accounts for longer.
  • More years of growth. Your balances compound for longer before you start drawing on them.
  • Fewer years of withdrawals. The same money has to cover a shorter retirement.
  • A shorter bridge. Fewer years separate your last paycheck from the point when guaranteed income such as Social Security begins, so there are fewer years that your savings must carry alone.

These effects add up, which is why retiring a few years later often looks much better on the chart than people expect. It is also why the first comparison people run, 60 versus 65 versus 70, tends to show big gaps. The gap is real, but it is also driven by your assumptions, so test it with a more cautious return.

Compare spending, not only age

Retirement age is only one lever. A second scenario with a modest reduction in yearly spending can sometimes close a gap that would take years of extra work to close otherwise. Try a baseline, then a version with spending lowered by a number you could actually live with, and see how the picture changes. Some people discover that a flexible spending plan is easier to achieve than three more years at a job they are tired of. Others discover that the gap is too large to fix with spending alone. Either answer is useful.

Read the success probability carefully

If you have the Monte Carlo feature, you will see a success rate for each scenario. This is the share of 1,000 randomized return sequences in which the money lasted through your projected lifespan. It is a probability, not a promise. A scenario with a high success rate is more resilient to bad market timing, but it is not guaranteed to work. The page on Monte Carlo simulation explains why two retirees with the same average return can end up in very different places because of the order in which returns arrive.

When you compare two scenarios, look at the gap between their success rates rather than only the level. A move from a middling rate to a much higher rate by working two more years says something concrete about how much that choice buys you. A move of a percentage point or two may not be worth a major life change.

Watch the withdrawal order

NestPlan draws on taxable accounts first, then tax deferred accounts, with Roth accounts held back for last, and it folds in required minimum distributions once they begin. This means a different retirement age can change when each type of account gets used, and therefore your tax picture in each year. If one scenario has unusually high taxes in a particular year, look at which account the money came from. The explanation is on the page about withdrawal order in retirement.

A worked way to organize your three scenarios

Here is a simple structure you can adapt.

  • Scenario A, earlier: your earliest realistic retirement age.
  • Scenario B, target: the age you are actually aiming for.
  • Scenario C, later: a few years past your target, as a safety valve.

Keep every other input identical across the three. Write down the result of each in a plain note: the age, the final balance, and the success rate if available. Then add a second round in which you lower the assumed return and repeat the exercise. If scenario B only works under the optimistic return, you have learned that your target age depends on markets cooperating, and you can decide how comfortable that makes you.

Saving and exporting your work

PRO supports up to 12 saved scenarios and lets you export reports as a PDF or back up your data as a JSON file. Everything is stored on your device, so exporting is how you keep a record if you change phones. Once you have settled on a scenario you like, an exported report is a useful thing to bring to a conversation with a financial professional.

Remember what the tool is not

NestPlan's own terms are clear that projections are estimates and not financial, tax or legal advice. Tax law changes, markets do not follow patterns, and personal circumstances shift. Use the sandbox to find out what matters, not to prove you are fine. The question to keep asking is: if one important assumption turns out wrong, what would I do?

Quick checklist

  • Build the best baseline you can first.
  • Copy those numbers into a second scenario and change only the retirement age.
  • Repeat the experiment for spending and for the assumed return.
  • Compare gaps between results, not only the headline numbers.
  • Export what you learn if you have PRO.
