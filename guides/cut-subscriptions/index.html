How to Audit and Cut Your Subscriptions

Money basics · By CoreForge LLC · Published November 5, 2026

Subscriptions are designed to be easy to start and easy to forget. A few dollars here and a few dollars there seem harmless, until you add them up across a year. The good news is that a subscription audit is one of the fastest ways to free up money, because you can often cut without changing anything important about your life. This guide gives you a method that takes about an evening.

Why subscriptions slip through

A one time purchase asks for a decision at the moment you pay. A subscription asks for a decision once, and then it quietly renews for as long as you let it. Three things make it worse:

  • Introductory offers that convert. A low price or a trial period turns into the full price automatically, and the reminder to cancel is on you.
  • Annual renewals. A yearly charge hits once, months after you signed up, so you have probably forgotten why you wanted it.
  • Small amounts. A charge that looks like pocket change does not feel worth a phone call, so it stays.

The point of the audit is not to cancel everything. It is to make sure every recurring charge is something you chose recently and still want.

Step 1: find every recurring charge

Do not rely on memory. Use the records.

  1. Bank and card statements. Open the last three months for every checking account and card. Highlight any charge that repeats at the same amount, or similar, on a regular schedule. Include accounts that belong to other members of your household if you share finances.
  2. Annual charges. Go back twelve months, or scan the year, to catch charges that appear only once. Software, memberships, domain names and insurance riders often renew yearly.
  3. App store subscriptions. On an iPhone, open Settings, tap your name, then tap Subscriptions. This list shows every active subscription billed through your device account, including some you may have forgotten.
  4. Email receipts. Search your inbox for words such as "receipt", "renewal", "subscription", "your plan" and "billing". Receipts often turn up services that bill a different card.
  5. Payment apps and online wallets. Check any service where you have stored a payment method, since subscriptions can hide behind them.

Write everything in one list with four columns: the name, the cost, how often it is charged, and the next charge date. Convert each to a yearly cost by multiplying monthly charges by twelve. A $9 monthly charge is $108 a year, and the yearly figure is the one that tends to get your attention.

Step 2: sort each one into a group

Go down the list and place every item in one of four groups.

  • Essential. Things you could not do without, such as a phone plan, home internet, insurance or a tool you need for work.
  • Valuable. Things you use often and enjoy enough that you would miss them.
  • Doubtful. Things you use sometimes but could live without.
  • Forgotten. Things you do not use or cannot remember signing up for.

Forgotten ones are the easy wins. Cancel them first. For doubtful ones, apply a test in the next step.

Step 3: apply a simple value test

For each doubtful subscription, ask these questions.

  1. When did I last use it? If you cannot answer, that tells you something.
  2. What is the cost per use? A $15 monthly service that you use twice a month costs $7.50 each time. If you use it twenty times a month, it costs 75 cents a time. The same price can be a bargain or a waste.
  3. Could I get the same value another way? A library card may cover books, audiobooks and movies. A shared household plan may be cheaper than several individual ones.
  4. Would I sign up again today at this price? If the honest answer is no, cancel.

When you are torn, there is a low risk compromise: cancel now, and if you truly miss it, sign up again. Most services let you return, and the months of savings usually exceed any sign up inconvenience. Some services even offer a better price to win you back.

Step 4: reduce instead of cancel

Some subscriptions are worth keeping but not at their current level. Look for these options:

  • Downgrade the plan. Drop to a cheaper tier, or one with ads, if the premium features are rarely used.
  • Switch to a shared or family plan. If several people in your household pay separately for the same kind of service, one shared plan may cost less.
  • Pause instead of cancel. Some services let you pause for a few months, which suits seasonal interests.
  • Rotate. Keep one streaming service at a time and switch each month or season, rather than paying for several at once.
  • Pay yearly if you are sure. A yearly plan is often cheaper than twelve monthly payments, but only if you would keep the service all year anyway.
  • Ask. For services such as phone, internet and insurance, call and ask whether a lower price or a better plan is available. A polite question sometimes saves money.

Step 5: cancel cleanly

Canceling is where many people get stuck, because some services make it deliberately awkward. A few habits help.

  • Cancel before the renewal date, with time to spare. Do it a few days ahead. Note the date on your calendar.
  • Cancel in the same place you signed up. A subscription started through your phone's store must be canceled through the phone's subscription settings, not on the service's website.
  • Save a confirmation. Take a screenshot or keep the confirmation email until you have seen the final charge disappear.
  • Check the next statement. If a charge appears after you canceled, contact the service with your confirmation. If it does not resolve, you can ask your card issuer about disputing it.
  • Remove the stored payment method. If you will not return, take your card off the account.

If you signed up for an introductory offer, put the end date in your calendar the same day. That one habit prevents the most common surprise charge.

Step 6: put the savings to work

If you do not give the money a job, it will quietly drift back into everyday spending. Decide where it goes right away. Good first choices include a starter emergency fund, extra payments on a high interest debt, or a specific goal. Add up the yearly savings from everything you canceled and set up an automatic monthly transfer for the same amount. Seeing a yearly figure grow in a savings account is far more motivating than knowing that you "cut a few subscriptions."

Keep creep from returning

  • Schedule a yearly audit. Put a repeating reminder on your calendar. Twenty minutes is usually enough once the first audit is done.
  • Use a one in, one out rule. Adding a new subscription means canceling an old one.
  • Wait a day. Before signing up for anything recurring, wait 24 hours. Most impulse sign ups fade.
  • Track recurring charges as their own category. A line labeled "subscriptions" in your budget keeps the total visible. See how to track your spending for ideas about categories.
  • Put renewal dates where you will see them. A list of upcoming bills that includes renewals helps you decide before a charge happens, not after.
