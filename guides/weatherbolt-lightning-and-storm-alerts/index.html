WeatherBolt Lightning Alerts and Storm Tracking Explained

WeatherBolt guides · By CoreForge LLC · Published November 5, 2026

Two of the most useful tools in WeatherBolt (formerly HarborCast) are also the two that deserve a careful explanation: the lightning layer with its alerts, and the storm tracker for hurricanes and tropical storms. Both are Pro features, with a short free preview of lightning. This guide explains what each one shows, how to choose settings that suit you, and where the limits are.

What the lightning layer shows

The lightning layer places recent strikes on the map. Each strike is colored to show how far it is from you and how long ago it happened, so you can tell a storm that is fresh and close from one that has moved off. Watching the layer over a few minutes gives you a sense of direction. If strikes are appearing in a line that gets closer to your position, the storm is approaching. If the newest strikes are farther away than the older ones, it is moving away.

The data comes from a satellite lightning detector run by NOAA, and the app notes that lightning location is approximate. That has a practical meaning. A strike shown 10 miles away may really be a bit nearer or farther, and the map is not a substitute for looking at the sky and listening for thunder. Use it as an early heads up, especially for the stretch of time before a storm is close enough to see or hear.

Setting up lightning alerts

Pro includes lightning alerts that work even when the app is closed. The feature is off by default and turns on only if you enable it. When you do, you choose an alert distance anywhere from 1 to 30 miles. You can also set quiet hours, so the phone does not wake you for storms at 3 a.m. when you are indoors and fine.

Choosing a distance

The right distance depends on what you are doing and how quickly you can reach shelter.

  • A larger distance, such as 20 to 30 miles, suits activities where it takes a long time to get safe, such as being on a boat, hiking far from a trailhead, or running an outdoor event with many people to move. It gives you the most warning and will also send more alerts.
  • A middle distance, perhaps 10 to 15 miles, is a reasonable starting point for everyday use around a home or a short outing.
  • A small distance, 1 to 5 miles, tells you that a storm is nearly on top of you. It works as a final confirmation, but it leaves little time to act, so most people should not rely on it alone.

If you are not sure, start with a larger distance for a week and see how often the alerts arrive. If it is too often, reduce it a little. It is easier to reduce an alert distance than to wish you had set it larger.

The simple 30 second rule for thunder, and the reasons not to wait until a storm "looks" close, are covered in Lightning Safety on the Water. An alert is best seen as a prompt to start your plan, not as the plan itself.

What happens to your data when alerts are on

Because alerts must reach you while the app is closed, the feature needs a small amount of information on a server. According to the WeatherBolt privacy policy, when you turn alerts on the app sends your device's Apple push notification token, an approximate city level alert location, your chosen alert radius and any quiet hours you set to an alert server run for CoreForge by Cloudflare. That information is used only to send you lightning alerts. It is not used for advertising or tracking, and it is removed when you turn lightning alerts off. If you prefer to keep everything on your device, you can leave the feature off and still use the lightning map when the app is open. Read the full text in the WeatherBolt privacy policy.

Making sure alerts reach you

If alerts are not arriving, work through the usual iPhone checks. Make sure notifications are allowed for the app in your device settings, that Focus modes are not silencing it, and that location permission is set in a way that works for the alert feature. Then check the quiet hours you set inside the app, since they can quietly explain a missing alert. Because the lightning data is satellite based and approximate, a strike near your chosen edge may not trigger an alert exactly when you expect.

The storm tracker

When a hurricane or tropical storm is active, the storm tracker shows its path, the cone of uncertainty, its past track and its forecast points. Each of these tells you something different.

  • The past track shows where the storm has been and how it has been moving, which gives context for its recent behavior.
  • The forecast points mark where the forecast expects the center to be at later times.
  • The cone represents the uncertainty in the center's path. It is not the size of the storm. A storm's wind, rain and surge can extend well beyond the cone, and a location outside the cone can still be affected.

That last point is the most commonly misunderstood feature of any storm map. The line through the middle is a best estimate of where the center might go, not a promise, and impacts can reach far from it. Use the tracker to understand the situation and then follow the guidance of local emergency officials and the National Weather Service for decisions about preparing or evacuating.

Storm alerts

Storm alerts tell you when a tropical storm's forecast path comes within a distance you pick, from 100 to 1000 miles. This is a very different scale from lightning alerts. A distance of a few hundred miles gives you days of lead time, enough to think about supplies, boats, travel and property. A distance near 1000 miles gives you the earliest possible notice, and it will also mean more alerts for storms that eventually miss you. Choose a larger distance if you have a boat to haul, a trip to reschedule or family in the area, and a smaller one if you only want to hear about storms that are genuinely close.

Putting the tools together

Here is a simple way to combine them across a season.

  1. Before storm season: turn on storm alerts with a distance that gives you comfortable lead time, and decide what you will do when one arrives.
  2. On a day with thunderstorm risk: turn on lightning alerts with a generous distance and make sure notifications are allowed.
  3. When an alert arrives: open the map, look at the direction of the strikes, and begin your plan to move to shelter.
  4. Afterward: remember that storms can circle back, so do not treat the first quiet minutes as an all clear. The 30 minute rule in the lightning safety guide still applies.

The limits you should keep in mind

WeatherBolt is an informational tool. The lightning data is approximate, the storm tracker reflects forecast information from official sources, and none of it replaces warnings from the National Weather Service or local officials. The same applies to anyone on the water, where the terms of the app say you should check official NOAA and Coast Guard advisories and not rely on the app as the sole basis for safety decisions.

Quick checklist

  • Pick a lightning alert distance that matches how long you need to reach shelter.
  • Set quiet hours so alerts do not wake you needlessly.
  • Remember that the cone shows path uncertainty, not storm size.
  • Pick a storm alert distance that gives you real preparation time.
  • Treat alerts as the start of a plan, not as the plan itself.
