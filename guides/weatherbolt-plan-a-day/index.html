Plan a Day on the Water with WeatherBolt

WeatherBolt guides · By CoreForge LLC · Published November 5, 2026

Good decisions about being on the water are mostly made before you leave the house. A repeatable routine takes the emotion out of the choice, especially on the days when you really want to go. This guide shows one way to use WeatherBolt (formerly HarborCast) for that routine, built around features the app actually has: your own limits, hourly Good, Caution and Danger ratings, optional tides and marine information, and the radar.

Step 1: decide your limits when you are calm

WeatherBolt rates each hour as Good, Caution or Danger using the numbers you set, and saving your own limits is a Pro feature. The best time to choose those numbers is not on the morning of a trip. It is on a quiet evening, with a clear head, thinking about the worst conditions you would still be comfortable in.

Think about each factor in terms of your boat or activity and your experience:

  • Wind and gusts. A steady wind and a gusty wind with the same average feel very different. The gust spread often matters more than the average, especially in a small boat. The guide on reading a marine forecast explains the difference.
  • Waves. Height matters, but so does the time between waves. A short, choppy sea is harder on a small boat than a long, rolling one with the same height.
  • Lightning and storms. Most people set a firm rule that thunderstorm risk means staying ashore or heading in.

Pick conservative numbers first. You can always loosen them later after you have more experience, but it is harder to tighten them after a bad day. A good test is to imagine the passenger who is least comfortable on your boat, and set your limits so that the plan works for them too.

The app's marine pages and terms are clear that it is an informational tool and not the only basis for navigation or safety decisions. Your limits are a personal planning aid, not a replacement for official advisories.

Step 2: turn on Tides & marine

If you are going out on the water, switch on Tides & marine in Settings. It stays off until you enable it, and it adds the tide chart, high and low tide times, the marine forecast and small craft advisories. You do not need these for a walk in the park, but for a boat, a kayak, a paddleboard or a fishing trip, they change the answer.

Step 3: scan the hourly ratings

Open the hourly forecast and look at the window you plan to be out, including the trip home. A day that is Good from 9 to noon and Caution from 2 onward is a morning trip, and the plan should include being back before the change. Write down the hour when conditions cross your line. That hour is your deadline, and having it written down makes it harder to talk yourself into staying out later.

Look at the trend, too. Hours that are drifting from Good toward Caution often mean a front or a sea breeze is building. Conditions that start worse and improve are a different story and may be worth waiting for.

Step 4: check the tide for your actual crossing

The tide chart and the times for high and low tide are especially important if you pass through an inlet, a narrow channel or a shallow area. Wind and swell against an outgoing tide can stack waves higher and steeper at an inlet mouth, so a forecast that looks fine overall can still be rough at the exact place you must cross. Check the tide at the time you will pass through, not just the general forecast. Tide times also tell you whether a ramp, a shallow cove or a sandbar will be usable when you plan to arrive and leave.

Step 5: read the marine forecast and any advisories

Look at the marine forecast and check for small craft advisories. An advisory is issued at published thresholds and tells you conditions have crossed a real line. It does not tell you whether your boat and experience are fine with that line, which is where your own limits come in. If an advisory covers your waters and your window, the sensible default is to postpone or choose a more sheltered location.

Step 6: watch the radar

Play back the last 2 hours of radar and note which way the rain and storms have been moving. Estimate how far away the nearest rain is and how quickly it is approaching. If you have Pro, open the lightning layer too. Strikes appear with their distance and age shown by color, which helps you tell a storm that is building from one that is fading. Lightning information is based on satellite detection and is approximate, so use it as an early heads up. Our guide on lightning safety on the water explains why you should head in earlier than feels necessary.

Step 7: set a go, no go and a turn around time

Turn your findings into three concrete statements.

  1. Go or no go. If any hour in your window is rated Danger, or an advisory covers your area, the answer is no go or a change of plan.
  2. Turn around time. The latest time you will start heading back, with enough buffer to reach shelter.
  3. Trigger to end early. What would make you leave sooner, such as a rating change, lightning within a distance you chose, or a wind that builds beyond your limit.

Tell someone ashore your plan and expected return, and carry the safety equipment your vessel and local rules require. Those steps are not part of any app, and they matter more than any forecast.

Step 8: use your wrist and widgets

WeatherBolt has an Apple Watch app with complications for wind, tide and a day rating clock, plus widgets for the Home Screen. On the water they save you from pulling out your phone. Add a complication for the factor that matters most to you, and glance at it every so often to check the trend against the plan you made on shore.

Review after the trip

After each outing, spend two minutes asking whether the forecast matched what you experienced and whether your limits felt right. If you were uncomfortable in conditions rated Good, lower your limits. If you stayed ashore on days that would have been fine, you may be able to loosen them a little, carefully. Over a season, the numbers you set will become a record of your own experience.

Quick checklist

  • Set conservative limits on a calm evening.
  • Turn on Tides & marine for any trip on the water.
  • Find the hour when ratings cross your line and treat it as a deadline.
  • Check the tide where you will actually cross.
  • Review radar, lightning and advisories before you leave.
  • Always tell someone your plan.
