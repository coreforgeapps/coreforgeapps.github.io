Getting Started with NestPlan: What to Gather First

NestPlan guides · By CoreForge LLC · Published November 5, 2026

NestPlan is a retirement calculator for iPhone that builds a year by year projection from your own accounts. A projection is only as good as the numbers you give it, so the best preparation happens before you open the app. This guide covers what to gather, what each number does inside the projection, and what you get with the Free and PRO versions.

What NestPlan is built to answer

The app is aimed at two questions that most people have but few can answer with confidence. First, when can I afford to retire? Second, if I do, will the money last? To answer them, NestPlan starts from your actual accounts, projects each balance forward year by year, models taxes and required minimum distributions, and draws money down in a sensible order once you stop working. Your inputs stay on your device, because NestPlan does not need an account or a login.

Step 1: list your accounts

NestPlan works with the account types most households actually hold:

  • Taxable brokerage accounts. Money you have already paid tax on. Withdrawals can trigger tax on the gains.
  • 401(k) or 403(b) accounts. Tax deferred workplace plans. Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.
  • Traditional IRAs. Also tax deferred, with similar treatment on the way out.
  • Roth IRAs. Money you paid tax on up front, which grows and comes out tax free if the account rules are followed.

The app also tracks HSA accounts and cost basis, and supports a spouse with separate accounts. Gather a recent statement for each account. You need the current balance, and you also need to know how much you expect to add each year until retirement. If you are a couple, collect both sets of numbers, because a plan that ignores one spouse's accounts will understate your options.

Keep the account types separate when you enter them. It can be tempting to combine everything into one total, but the reason NestPlan asks for the types is that they are taxed differently. A single lump of "retirement money" hides the very thing that decides how much tax you pay on the way out.

Step 2: gather income that arrives later

Social Security and pension income reduce how much you need to pull from your savings. Collect the estimates you have, such as the statement from the Social Security Administration and any pension letter from a former employer. These are estimates, and they change with the age at which you start collecting. If you are unsure, use a cautious number and run the plan again with a different one. A plan that still works with a conservative estimate is more reassuring than one that only works with an optimistic estimate.

Step 3: decide on your assumptions

NestPlan asks for an assumed rate of return and factors in inflation for both your expenses and your income. Two honest points about these assumptions:

  • No one knows what returns will be. A projection that uses a single steady rate is a useful baseline, not a forecast.
  • Small changes compound. An assumed return that is a point higher can make a plan look comfortable that is actually fragile. Try a lower number and see what happens.

The app's Monte Carlo mode is its answer to this uncertainty, because it runs 1,000 randomized return sequences instead of relying on one rate. You can read how it works in What Monte Carlo Simulation Actually Tells You.

Step 4: estimate what you will spend

Retirement spending is the number people guess at most and the one that moves the result most. A practical way to estimate it is to look at what you spend today, remove the costs that will end (such as commuting, retirement contributions and a mortgage that will be paid off), and add the costs that will begin or grow, such as health care and travel. Review a year of statements rather than relying on memory. If you track your spending in a budgeting tool, the yearly totals by category give you a good starting point. Our guide to tracking your spending shows one simple way to do that.

What the projection shows

Once your numbers are in, NestPlan builds a year by year picture of your balances through retirement. When you reach the point of drawing money down, it follows a specific order rather than pulling evenly from every account: taxable accounts first, then tax deferred accounts, with Roth accounts held back for last. Once you reach the age when required minimum distributions begin, the app calculates the mandatory withdrawal from your tax deferred accounts and folds it into that year's income, so the tax picture reflects what actually has to happen. If you want the reasoning, read Withdrawal Order in Retirement.

Free and PRO

According to the app's own terms and support pages, the two versions differ like this.

The Free version includes

  • Basic retirement projections with tax calculations
  • HSA tracking and cost basis calculations
  • Spouse support with separate accounts
  • One saved scenario

PRO adds

  • Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 runs
  • The Total Assets Over Time chart and the Asset Allocation chart
  • A year by year projection table
  • PDF export with full reports
  • JSON import and export for backups
  • Up to 12 saved scenarios

Pricing is shown inside the app, and subscriptions are managed through your Apple Account. If you are just getting oriented, the Free version is enough to build a first projection and see how the tax treatment affects your result. If you want to test your plan against bad market luck or compare several retirement ages, PRO is the version that supports it.

Where your data lives

Everything you enter is stored locally on your device. That is a real convenience, since there is nothing to sign up for. It also means that deleting the app removes your saved data. If you are on PRO, use the export option to keep a backup before you delete or replace a device.

Read the result with a skeptical eye

NestPlan is a planning tool, not financial, tax or legal advice. The app itself says its tax calculations are simplified and that actual outcomes may differ from projections. Use the output to find out which questions to ask, and talk to a qualified professional before making a major decision. The most useful result of a first run is often not the headline number but the discovery of which assumption the plan depends on most.

A short checklist before you start

  • A recent balance for each account, with its type
  • Expected yearly contributions until you stop working
  • Estimated Social Security and any pension income
  • A realistic yearly spending figure for retirement
  • A cautious assumed return, plus a second, lower one to compare
